Which export markets will collapse next year?

An early-warning model for India's exports. For every product group India sells to every country, it uses only what is known at the end of a year to estimate the chance that the flow falls by more than a quarter (or stops) the next year. It is tested the honest way: year by year, always predicting a year it has never seen.

In brief

The answer in brief

  • Watch list: each year the model gives every flow a risk score from 0 to 1. The 10% of flows with the highest scores form the watch list: the short list someone should check first. Its hit rate is the share of the list that really collapsed.
  • Question: using only what is known at the end of a year, can I tell which of India's export flows (one product group to one country) will fall by more than about a quarter, or stop, the next year?
  • Data: 131,550 flows (206 countries, 96 product groups, 1996-2023), built from CEPII BACI trade data, CEPII Gravity and World Bank GDP and population.
  • Key fact about the data: about 22% of flows collapse the next year, and a simple rule that flags the jumpiest flows already does well (AUC 0.69). That rule is the bar the model has to clear.
  • Result: boosted trees beat the rule in all 10 walk-forward years (AUC 0.731 vs 0.694) and in all 4 post-COVID years with the model frozen (0.733 vs 0.701). Resampling whole countries, the 95% range of the gain is +0.031 to +0.043. About 54% of the model's watch list really collapses, against 47% for the rule and 23% for random picking.
  • Where it fails: steady flows hit by a sudden drop in the buyer's demand, and "lumpy" products (fuel, gems, aircraft, ships) that crowd the watch list. For the biggest flows (over $100M) it is no better than the rule.
  • Honest caveats: many "collapses" are spikes falling back (27% of collapses only undo that year's jump), most of the model's skill comes from the flow's own history (a tree on those 7 inputs alone gets AUC 0.722), and BACI data arrives about a year late, so in real use the warning would come late.
  • What it is not: the scores are early-warning signals, not causes.
1 · Problem formulation

The question

Using only what is known at the end of year t, which of India's export flows will collapse in year t+1?

One rowIndia's exports of one product group (2-digit HS code, e.g. "30 Pharmaceuticals") to one country in one year.
Target: collapseNext year's value falls by more than 26% (log change below −0.3) or drops to zero.
Prediction timeEnd of year t, as if year-t data were already available. Every input uses year t or earlier; next year's numbers are never used. In reality BACI data for a year is published about 13 months after the year ends, so a real warning would arrive after most of year t+1 has passed. The test measures how well the pattern predicts, not how early a real user would know.
OutputA risk score from 0 to 1 for each flow. Sorting all flows by risk and taking the top 10% gives the watch list: the flows to check first, because a real team cannot check every flow.
Try it: would this count as a collapse?
Type in a flow's value this year and next year (in $ millions) and see how the project labels it.

Prediction vs decision

The model predicts a risk score. It does not decide anything. The decision it supports is where to look first: an exporter, an export-credit insurer, or a trade-promotion office has limited time and could review the top 10% of flows first, checking the buyer's situation before shipping on credit or before the market is lost. Whether a warning is worth acting on depends on the size of the flow and the cost of acting, which the model does not know.

Limits of the claim

The scores are predictive associations, not causes. A high score does not explain why a flow will fall, and the model cannot foresee shocks that leave no trace in past data (a sudden ban, a war, a new tariff).

Quality checks and choices

  • Zeros were added on purpose. BACI lists only trades that happened. Without the zero years, a flow that stopped completely would just vanish, and the worst collapses would never be in the data.
  • Only flows of $1M or more. Tiny flows jump around randomly; a $20,000 shipment doubling or vanishing is noise, not a signal.
  • Check numbers were compared at every step (e.g. India's 2015 exports total $278.8bn in BACI; the Nigeria medicines flow in 2015 is $338.7M).
  • Gaps: World Bank GDP for the latest year is often not published yet, so it is left blank and the models handle blanks. The Gravity file ends in 2021, so later years carry the last known value forward (distance and language do not change).

3 · Preprocessing and statistical reasoning

Preprocessing: 24 inputs, all known at the end of year t

Trade values are hugely skewed ($1M next to $10bn), so values are put on a log scale, and changes are log changes (log of this year minus log of last year). A log change of −0.3 means a fall of about 26%, and rises and falls of the same size become symmetric.

Every input was chosen before testing, from a simple economic idea of why a trade flow might fall. I did not search through hundreds of possible inputs to find the ones that happened to score best, because that can produce patterns that are just luck.

The flow's own history (7 inputs)

The best guide to a flow's future is often its own past.

InputWhat it isWhy it was chosen
SizeThe flow's value that year (log scale)Big, established flows are usually more stable than small ones.
Change this year, last year, 2 years agoHow much the flow rose or fell in each of the last 3 yearsA flow that just jumped often falls back; one that keeps falling may keep falling. Three years was enough: 5 or 7 years of changes improved AUC by only about 0.001.
VolatilityHow much the flow swung up and down over the last 5 years (spread of the yearly changes)Flows that swing a lot are more likely to have a big drop. 5 years is a trade-off: with yearly data, fewer years gives too few numbers for a steady estimate, and more years lets old swings that no longer matter count. It is the same window often used for a stock's beta (5 years, or 60 months, of returns). The window was fixed before testing, not tuned.
3-year trendThe overall change over the last 3 yearsSeparates a flow that is slowly shrinking from one that is growing.
Years traded (of last 5)In how many of the last 5 years India sold this product to this country at allNew or on-and-off flows are fragile and stop easily.

The buyer's market (6 inputs)

A flow can fall because the buyer wants less, or because the buyer switches to other sellers.

InputWhat it isWhy it was chosen
Buyer's imports of this productHow much the country's imports of this product (from everyone) grewIf the country buys less of the product overall, India's sales will likely fall too.
Buyer's total importsHow much the country's imports of everything grewA sign of a wider slowdown or crisis in the buying country.
India's market share, and its changeIndia's share of the country's imports of this product, and how it movedA falling share means other sellers are winning the market.
India's exports of this product to all countriesHow India's sales of this product to the whole world changedSeparates a problem with this buyer from a problem with India's supply.
Buyer's weight in India's exportsHow important this country is among India's buyersLarge, important partners tend to be more stable relationships.

The buying country (8 inputs)

Slow-moving facts about the buyer and its link to India.

InputWhat it isWhy it was chosen
GDP growthHow fast the buyer's economy grewA shrinking economy buys less.
Size of economy, populationThe buyer's share of world GDP and population (log)Small economies have smaller, less stable markets.
DistanceDistance between India and the buyerFar-away trade is costlier and easier to drop.
Shared border, shared languageWhether the two countries share a border or an official languageClose, familiar partners tend to keep trading.
WTO membership, trade agreementWhether the buyer is in the WTO and has a trade agreement with IndiaTrade rules and agreements make sudden barriers less likely.

India's other flows (3 inputs)

Collapses tend to come in groups.

InputWhat it isWhy it was chosen
Other products to this buyer that fellShare of India's other products to this country that fell sharply this yearIf many flows to one country are falling, that country may be in trouble.
Average change of other products to this buyerAverage change of India's other products to this countryThe same idea, measured as an average.
This product falling in other countriesShare of other countries where this same product fell sharplyIf a product falls everywhere, the problem is the product, not the buyer.

The row's own value is always left out of these three, so the model never sees its own number twice. The ablation in Results shows how much each group adds.

Avoiding leakage

  • Every input is computed from year t or earlier. Next year's value is used only to make the answer (the target).
  • The only learned preprocessing (filling blanks with the median and scaling, for logistic regression) sits inside a scikit-learn pipeline, so it is fitted on training years only. The volatility rule's fill value also comes from training years only.
  • The test years are never shuffled into training (see Evaluation).
4 · Baseline and method selection

Baselines and methods

MethodWhat it doesWhy it is here
RandomPicks flows at random.The floor. Its watch-list hit rate is just the collapse rate (about 23%).
Jump ruleRanks flows by this year's change, biggest rise first. No learning.A second simple baseline: many of the riskiest flows are ones that just jumped, so I check whether this alone does as well.
Volatility ruleRanks flows by their volatility (how much they swung over the last 5 years). No learning.The honest baseline: the first thing a trade analyst would try. Any model must beat this to be worth it.
Logistic regressionA weighted sum of the inputs, turned into a probability (L2 penalty, C = 1). Blanks are filled with the median and every input is standardised (mean 0, spread 1) inside a pipeline, so the scaling is learned from training years only.Simple, readable, but cannot see interactions (e.g. "jumpy and the buyer is shrinking").
Boosted trees (scikit-learn HistGradientBoosting)300 small trees, each fixing the mistakes of the ones before (learning rate 0.05).Main model. Handles skewed inputs, blanks and interactions without hand-made features. No scaling is needed: a split like "volatility above 0.4" works the same at any scale.
XGBoostThe same idea from another library (300 trees, depth 5, learning rate 0.05).A check that the result is not one library's quirk. Also gives exact per-input reasons (SHAP).

Boosted trees are the main model for all test results, including the "Check a past year" tool. XGBoost was tested in exactly the same way (AUC 0.735 vs 0.731). It is used for the 2025 watch list only because it gives exact reasons for each flow, not because it scored higher.

Deep learning was left out on purpose: the inputs are a small table of numbers, where tree ensembles are the standard strong choice, and the course question is about evaluation, not model size.

5 · Evaluation design and metrics

Evaluation: always predict a year the model has never seen

A random train/test split would let the model learn from 2019 to "predict" 2012. That is not possible in real life, so it would give an over-optimistic score. Instead:

See the test for any year
Drag the slider to pick the year being predicted. Each box is one year of data. Blue rows train the model, the orange row is what it is tested on, and grey rows are the future it never sees.
Example. To predict which flows collapse in 2013, the model is trained only on rows from 2011 and earlier, because their outcomes (2012 and before) were already known at the end of 2012. It is then given each flow's 2012 numbers and asked which will collapse in 2013, and its answers are compared with what really happened. The 2012 rows are not used for training here, because their outcome is 2013, the year being predicted; they join the training data one step later, to predict 2014. This is repeated for every year 2011-2020, and the reported score is the average of these 10 years.
  • Frozen post-COVID test (answer years 2021-2024): COVID disrupted world trade, so I check whether a model built on the pre-COVID world still works. The model is trained once on all rows whose outcome was known by 2020, then frozen: it is never retrained or adjusted. That same model predicts 2021, 2022, 2023 and 2024, and the reported score is the average of these 4 years. None of these years was used for any choice, so this is the cleanest test.
  • No tuning on test years. Model settings were fixed sensible values chosen before testing and kept the same for every year. The one design choice explored (how many past changes to use: 1, 3, 5 or 7) was checked on the walk-forward years only; the differences were tiny (AUC 0.729-0.731), so the simple choice (3) was kept. The 2021-2024 years were not used for any choice.

Metrics, and why

  • AUC: pick one flow that collapsed and one that did not; how often does the method rank the collapsed one higher? 0.5 = coin flip. It does not depend on a threshold, and it is fair across years whose collapse rates differ.
  • Top-10% hit rate: of the 10% riskiest flows (the watch list), what share really collapsed? This matches the decision: a team that can review only a tenth of the flows.
  • PR-AUC and Brier score (how close probabilities are to what happened) are reported too. Accuracy is not used: with 78% of flows not collapsing, "nothing collapses" is already 78% accurate.
  • Uncertainty: flows to the same country move together, so I resample whole countries 500 times (bootstrap) and recompute the AUC gain over the rule.

That is a real but small gain, and I report it as such.

What these results show

  • The model beats the main baseline in every year: all 10 walk-forward years and all 4 frozen post-COVID years. It still works after COVID without retraining.
  • The gain is real but modest: about 0.04 AUC over the volatility rule on average.
  • The watch list is right about half the time, more than twice as often as random picking. With about 600 flows on the list each year, 54% instead of 47% means roughly 40 more real collapses caught per year for the same amount of checking.

9 · Limitations

Limitations

  • The gain is real but modest. AUC 0.73 vs 0.69 for a simple rule. About half of the model's correct warnings are flows the rule also flags.
  • Almost no gain for the biggest flows. For flows over $100M, the model's AUC is 0.753 and the volatility rule's is 0.752. These are the flows that matter most to an exporter, so for them the simple rule is about as good.
  • The watch list misses most collapses. A list of 10% of flows can only hold part of the roughly 22% that collapse; it caught about a quarter of all collapses.
  • 2011-2020 was used both to develop and to report. Settings were fixed in advance and only one small design choice was checked on these years, but the frozen 2021-2024 test is the cleanest result.
  • Many collapses are spikes falling back. A collapse is measured against this year's value, so a flow that jumps 4× and then gives back a quarter counts as a collapse. 27% of all collapses end at or above the level of two years earlier (they only undo that year's jump), and 30% came right after the flow at least doubled. So the label mixes real lost markets with jumps falling back, and much of the watch list is the second kind. A target measured against a 3-year average would separate them; I leave that for future work.
  • The 2025 list uses blank GDP. 2024 GDP was not yet published when the data was built, so both GDP inputs are blank for every 2024 flow, while they are almost always filled in training. This can push the 2025 risk scores up, so the 2025 list is best read as a ranking. Carrying 2023 GDP forward (as I already do for the Gravity data) would fix it.
  • Risk scores are not checked percentages. The scores were not calibrated, so "70%" should be read as "high on the ranking", not as an exact probability.
  • Blind to new shocks. Bans, wars, sanctions and sudden policy changes leave no trace in past trade, so steady flows hit by them are missed.
  • 2-digit product groups are coarse. "Machinery" mixes very different goods; a collapse in one part can be hidden by growth in another.
  • The 26% cut-off is a choice. A different line gives a different question. The table also keeps a stricter target (the flow halves or stops), left for future work.
  • Data revisions and lags. BACI is reconciled from countries' reports and revised over time; in real use the latest year arrives with a delay, and the latest GDP may be missing.
  • Only India. The model is trained and tested on India's exports; it may not carry over to other exporters without retraining.
10 · Conclusion

Conclusion

Next-year collapses of India's export flows are partly predictable from information available at the end of the year. A boosted-tree model beats the honest baseline (the volatility rule) in every one of 10 walk-forward years and in all 4 post-COVID years with the model frozen, and the gain holds when whole countries are resampled. Its watch list is right about half the time, more than twice as often as random picking.

The model is most useful as a first filter for where to look, not as a final answer: it catches jumpy and shrinking flows well, but it misses steady flows hit by sudden shocks from the buyer, and its list is crowded by lumpy products such as fuel, gems and aircraft unless those are shown separately.

11 · Reproducibility

Reproduce it

  1. Download the BACI HS92 V202601 zip and the Gravity V202211 zip from CEPII into your Google Drive (My Drive).
  2. Open notebooks/export_collapse_full.ipynb in Google Colab and run all cells. It builds the table (check: 131,550 rows, collapse share 0.219) and runs the walk-forward and frozen tests.
  3. Run notebooks/analysis_and_website_data.ipynb. It makes the error analysis, the bootstrap, the ablation, the 2025 watch list and the JSON files in docs/data/.

Where the numbers come from

Every number on this site was produced by running the code in the two notebooks. Nothing was typed in by hand. The notebooks are saved with their outputs, so each result can be seen on GitHub printed under the code that produced it:

  • export_collapse_full.ipynb, Steps 1-8: building the data table, with check numbers.
  • export_collapse_full.ipynb, Step 11: walk-forward results (2011-2020) for the volatility rule, logistic regression, boosted trees and XGBoost.
  • export_collapse_full.ipynb, Step 12: frozen post-COVID results, year by year 2021-2024.
  • analysis_and_website_data.ipynb, Step 4: random guessing, the jump rule, year-by-year scores and the bootstrap.
  • analysis_and_website_data.ipynb, Steps 5-7: which inputs help, the error analysis and the 2025 watch list.

The charts read their numbers from docs/data/, which the second notebook writes. The outputs saved in the notebooks come from a second run on Google Colab with slightly different library versions. Overall averages differ only in the third decimal (for example AUC 0.730 vs 0.731, 4,417 vs 4,408 collapses caught), but single-year watch-list hit rates can differ by up to about 2 points. This changes one count: the model beats the jump rule's watch list in 11 of 14 years in the site's run and 13 of 14 in the saved notebooks. The main conclusions are the same.

Fixed random seed 0 for all models. No data files are stored in the repository except the small result files the site reads. Full steps, citations and the AI-use note are in the README.
